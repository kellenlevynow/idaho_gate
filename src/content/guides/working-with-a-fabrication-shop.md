## The five things a shop needs

### 1. What it is and what it does

"A bracket" tells us little. "A bracket that holds a 200 pound hose reel on a concrete wall, outdoors" tells us the load, the environment and the mounting. Function lets us catch problems the drawing does not show.

### 2. Dimensions

Overall size, hole locations and sizes, and anything it has to fit around or bolt to. Mark which dimensions are critical. If it mates with an existing part, send that part's dimensions too, or the part.

### 3. Material

Steel, aluminum or stainless, and a thickness if you know it. If you do not, describe the load and let us recommend.

### 4. Finish

Raw, primed, painted, powder coated or galvanized. Indoors or out. A color if it matters.

### 5. Quantity and date

How many, and when you need them. If more may follow, say so. It changes how we build the first one.

## Ways to describe a part

From best to still workable:

1. **A CAD file.** DXF or DWG for flat parts, STEP for 3D.
2. **A dimensioned drawing.** Hand-drawn is fine if it is clear.
3. **A sketch with key sizes.** We redraw it.
4. **The original part.** We measure and reproduce it.
5. **Photos with a tape in frame.** Good for a first estimate.

## What changes the price

**Material.** Type, thickness and how much is wasted in cutting. Standard sizes cost less than specials.

**Cutting.** Simple straight cuts are fast. Complex profiles go on the CNC table, which needs a drawing but then repeats perfectly.

**Forming and fitting.** Bends, rolled curves and coped joints take setup and skill.

**Welding.** Length and type of weld, access, and whether welds need to be ground smooth or left as laid.

**Tolerance.** Holding a part to a sixteenth of an inch is routine. Holding it much tighter over a long length takes fixtures, checking and sometimes machining.

**Finish.** Each step up in protection adds cost and lead time. Galvanizing and powder coating are done in batches on their own schedule.

**Quantity.** The first piece carries the setup. Each one after it costs less.

**Engineering.** If a part carries a significant load or needs a permit, a licensed engineer's design or review is part of the job.

## What you should get back

- **A written quote** listing material, fabrication, finish and any engineering, with exclusions stated.
- **Questions.** A shop that asks none either understood everything or assumed everything.
- **A shop drawing** for anything beyond the simplest part.
- **A lead time** that includes finishing.

## Approving a shop drawing

The shop drawing is what will be built. Check it against what you need, not what you sent.

- Overall dimensions and hole patterns.
- Material and thickness.
- Which way it faces and which side is seen.
- Finish and color.
- Quantity.

If something is wrong, now is the time. After cutting, changes cost real money.

## Questions worth asking any shop

1. Have you built something like this before?
2. Does this need an engineer?
3. What would you change to make it better or cheaper?
4. Who does the finishing, and is it in the lead time?
5. Do you deliver or install?
6. What do you need from me to start?
