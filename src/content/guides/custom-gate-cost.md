## Why there is no simple answer

A custom gate is built for one opening. Its price is the sum of design, material, shop hours, finish, site work, hardware and automation. Change the width by four feet or the infill from pickets to plate and several of those move at once.

What we can do is show you exactly what moves the number, so you can decide where to spend.

## The nine cost drivers

### 1. Size

Width and height set how much steel is in the gate. They also set leaf weight, which sets post size, footing size, hinge rating and operator rating. Cost climbs faster than width.

### 2. How it opens

A single swing leaf is the simplest. A double swing adds a second leaf, hinge set and operator. A cantilever slide adds the tail, support posts, roller trucks and an operator pad.

### 3. Material

Steel is the baseline. Aluminum costs more per pound but uses fewer pounds and can reduce operator and post costs. Weathering steel carries a premium over mild steel. Timber infill adds material and fitting time.

### 4. Infill and detail

Straight pickets are fast to build. Tight horizontal slats take more material and more fitting. Rolled arches, cut panels, layered plate and forged details add design and shop hours. Custom artwork adds drawing time.

### 5. Posts, footings and pillars

Steel posts in properly sized footings are a fixed need. Stone or brick pillars are a separate trade and a meaningful part of an estate entrance budget.

### 6. Finish

In rising order of cost and life: paint, powder coat over primer, hot-dip galvanizing, galvanizing plus powder coat. On a gate that should last decades, finish is not the place to economize.

### 7. Automation

The operator or operators, battery backup, monitored safety devices, entry devices, exit sensors, locks and setup. A basic residential package and a full intercom and app system are far apart.

### 8. Power

A short low-voltage run from a nearby garage is modest. A long trench across a paved drive, a new circuit, or a solar system sized for winter each add their own cost.

### 9. Site work

Grading a swing area, cutting and patching pavement for loops and conduit, rock in the footing holes, difficult access, and tying into existing fences all show up here. This is the line most likely to surprise, which is why we look at the site before quoting.

## Where the money goes on a typical job

Without putting figures on it, an automated residential entrance usually divides into four roughly comparable blocks: the gate itself, the posts and site work, the automation, and the finishing touches such as pillars and lighting. Skip the fourth block and you have a complete working gate. Skip the third and you have a manual gate that is ready to automate.

## How to compare two quotes

Quotes for "a 16 foot automated gate" can describe very different things. Check each one for:

- **Post size and footing depth.** Is it stated?
- **Frame material and wall thickness.**
- **Finish system.** Blasted and primed, or powder over bare steel? Galvanized?
- **Operator make, model and rating** against the gate's weight.
- **Safety devices.** How many, what type, monitored?
- **Battery backup and surge protection.**
- **Power.** Who runs it, and is it included?
- **Permits, locates and cleanup.**
- **A drawing.** Did you get one?

The lower quote is often missing two or three of these.

## Ways to control cost without regret

- **Simplify the infill**, not the structure.
- **Phase it.** Gate and conduit now, automation and pillars later.
- **Choose a standard finish color.**
- **Keep the opening no wider than you need**, but do not go narrow to save money and then scrape a mirror every week.
- **Decide early.** Changes after fabrication starts are the most expensive kind.
