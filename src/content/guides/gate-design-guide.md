## Step 1: Read the site

Stand in the driveway and answer six questions. Write the answers down. They are the brief.

1. **How wide is the opening**, and what is the widest vehicle that uses it?
2. **Does the ground rise or fall** inside the gate, across the gate, or both?
3. **How far is the gate from the road?** Can a vehicle stop fully off the road while it opens?
4. **Is there clear room to swing inward**, or a level run along the fence to slide?
5. **Where does plowed snow go**, and where does it drift?
6. **Where is power**, and how far is it?

Our [measuring guide](/resources/measuring-your-opening/) shows how to take these down in ten minutes.

## Step 2: Choose how it opens

There are two families.

**Swing gates** hinge on posts and sweep an arc. They are simple, quiet and usually less expensive. They need level ground through the whole arc and room for it.

**Sliding gates** run along the fence line. They need no swing room, handle slopes and wind better, and suit wide openings. They need a level run-back roughly one and a half times the opening for a cantilever design.

If your drive climbs away from the gate, or you cannot keep an arc clear in winter, you are probably looking at a slide. The full comparison is in [swing vs sliding gates](/resources/swing-vs-sliding-gates/).

## Step 3: Set the size

**Width** follows the vehicles: cars, trailers, delivery trucks, the plow, and fire apparatus if the drive is long or shared. Wider is rarely regretted. Narrower often is.

**Height** follows purpose and proportion. A four-foot ranch gate says "private." A six-foot picket gate keeps most people out. A seven-foot solid gate gives real privacy and needs real structure.

## Step 4: Get the structure right

This is the part you do not see in photos, and it is the part that decides whether the gate works in year fifteen.

- **Posts.** Steel, sized to the leaf, set in concrete below frost depth.
- **Frame.** Welded tube, heavier on the hinge side, braced on wide leaves.
- **Hinges or rollers.** Rated for the weight, with margin, and serviceable.
- **Latching.** A positive stop and a latch or lock that holds in wind.

A rule of thumb: every choice that adds weight or wind area in the next step sends you back here to step things up.

## Step 5: Pick the infill

Infill is what fills the frame. It sets the look, the privacy, the weight and the wind load.

| Infill | Look | Privacy | Wind load | Weight |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Vertical pickets | Traditional, open | Low | Low | Low |
| Horizontal slats | Modern | Medium to high | Medium | Medium |
| Rails and brace | Ranch | Low | Low | Medium |
| Sheet or plate | Clean, solid | Full | High | High |
| Timber in steel | Warm | High | High | High |
| Cut panels | Custom feature | Varies | Varies | Medium |

You can mix them. Pickets above a cut panel, as on the concept gate on this page, gives a feature at car height and openness above.

## Step 6: Choose the finish

- **Powder coat** over a zinc-rich primer is the standard. Durable, any color.
- **Hot-dip galvanizing** protects inside and out. Add powder coat for color.
- **Weathering steel** forms its own patina and needs no coating.
- **Aluminum** will not rust and can be coated or left bare.

More in the [materials guide](/resources/materials-guide/).

## Step 7: Decide on automation

Even if you plan to open it by hand for now, decide what you might add.

- **Operator.** Sized to the leaf and the daily use.
- **Entry.** Keypad, remote, phone, intercom.
- **Exit.** A free-exit sensor so leaving is effortless.
- **Safety.** Monitored photo eyes or edges. Not optional on an automated gate.
- **Power.** Utility, low voltage from the house, or solar.

Then have conduit placed before any concrete is poured. Read the [automation guide](/resources/gate-automation-guide/) for the details.

## Step 8: Finish the entrance

Pillars, lighting, an address plate and a pedestrian gate turn a gate into an entrance. They are easiest and cheapest to include at the design stage.
