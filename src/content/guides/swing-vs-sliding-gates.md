## The short version

Choose a **swing gate** if the ground inside the gate is level, you have room for the leaves to open, and you can keep that area clear in winter.

Choose a **sliding gate** if the drive rises inside the gate, space is tight, the opening is very wide, the gate is solid, the site is windy, or snow makes a clear arc unrealistic.

## Side by side

| | Swing | Cantilever slide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Space needed | An arc inside the gate as long as each leaf | A level run beside the opening, about 1.5 times its width |
| Sloped driveway | Needs level ground through the arc | Fine, if the run along the fence is level |
| Snow | Arc must be kept plowed | Nothing on the ground. Run-back kept clear |
| Wind | Loads the hinge post and operator | Load shared by two posts along the fence line |
| Wide openings | Two long leaves, large loads | One long panel. Scales well |
| Mechanical parts | Hinges | Rollers, guides, receiver |
| Typical cost | Lower | Higher |
| Look | Traditional or modern | Modern or utilitarian, can be dressed up |

## Space

Draw it on the ground. For a swing gate, mark the hinge point and sweep the length of the leaf inward. Everything inside that arc must stay empty: no parked car, no rising slope, no boulder, no snow pile. The plan on this page shows the arc for a concept single swing gate.

For a slide, measure along the fence line on the side the gate will run. You need the opening width plus the tail, level and clear of trees, utilities and buildings.

## Slope

A swing leaf moves in a flat plane. If the drive climbs inside the gate, the bottom of the leaf hits the ground partway through. You can raise the gate, which leaves a gap when closed. You can use rising hinges or rake the bottom rail. Or you can slide.

A sliding gate does not care what the driveway does. It only needs its own path to be level.

## Snow and ice

In snow country this often settles it. A swing gate pushes against whatever is in its arc. A few inches of wet snow can stop it, and an operator that keeps pushing against a drift wears out early.

A cantilever slide hangs above the driveway on rollers mounted to posts. There is no track to pack with ice. Keep the run-back strip clear and it works.

A V-track slide rolls on a rail in the pavement. That rail collects ice and plow gravel. We generally steer North Idaho sites to cantilever.

More in [gates in snow and ice](/resources/gates-in-snow-and-ice/).

## Wind

A gate leaf is a sail. A swing gate carries wind as a twisting load on one hinge post and as a push on the operator. A solid swing gate in an open, windy spot can stall or slam.

A sliding gate is held at two support posts and, when closed, at the receiver. It takes wind far better, which is why privacy gates so often slide.

## Cost and complexity

For the same opening, a swing gate has less steel and less hardware. A slide has a longer panel, two support posts, roller trucks, top guides, a receiver and an operator pad. Expect it to cost more.

That said, compare total cost. If a swing gate needs the drive regraded, or will need rescuing every snowstorm, the slide may be the cheaper gate to own.

## Safety

Both need monitored entrapment protection when automated. Sliding gates have additional construction rules: covered rollers, screening next to the travel path and limits on gaps. See [safety and compliance](/automation/safety-and-compliance/).
