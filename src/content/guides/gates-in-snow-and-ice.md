## What winter does to a gate

- **Adds weight.** Snow and ice on the leaf and wind behind it raise the load on the operator.
- **Blocks the path.** A swing arc fills. A track packs with ice.
- **Stiffens everything.** Grease thickens, seals harden, hinges drag.
- **Weakens batteries.** Capacity drops in the cold just when the gate needs more.
- **Moves the ground.** Frost can lift a shallow footing and put a gate in a bind.
- **Hides things.** Sensors, loops, stops and keypads get buried or iced over.

## Design decisions that solve it

### Pick the right type

A cantilever sliding gate keeps its entire mechanism above the ground. For sites with deep or drifting snow, steep drives, or owners who are away, it is the most forgiving design. The drawing on this page is a concept.

A swing gate is fine where the arc can be kept clear. Give it honest ground clearance, more than you would in a mild climate, and accept the gap at the bottom.

Avoid tracks in the pavement and in-ground operators unless the area is covered or heated and drains well.

### Plan the snow before the gate

Ask where the plow pushes. That pile must not be in the swing arc, the slide run-back, in front of the photo eyes, on the operator, or on the keypad. Sometimes moving the gate ten feet solves it. Sometimes it means choosing which side the gate slides to.

Then ask where it drifts. Fences, banks and buildings create drifts on their downwind side.

### Set footings below frost

A post footing that sits above the frost line gets lifted a little each winter and does not always settle back. Over a few years the gate goes out of line. Footings go below local frost depth, and wider at the base.

### Keep devices up and reachable

Mount photo eyes high enough to stay above snow and where they can be wiped. Put the keypad where the plow will not bury it and a driver can still reach it past a snowbank. Protect the operator from plow strikes with a bollard.

### Let water out

Every tube needs a drain hole at the bottom. Locks, hinge barrels and roller housings should shed water. Meltwater that gets in on a sunny afternoon freezes that night.

## Equipment choices

- **Cold-rated lubricants** on hinges, rollers and operators, as the manufacturer specifies.
- **Operator rated for the temperature.** Check the published operating range.
- **Battery sized for cold.** And replaced before it is weak, not after.
- **Heater kit** where offered and where power allows.
- **Solar sized for December.** Steep panel angle to shed snow.

## Running the gate through winter

1. **Before the first snow.** Clean and lubricate. Test safety devices. Check the battery. Clear drain holes. Mark the edges of the drive and any sensors.
2. **After each storm.** Clear the gate's path before cycling it. Brush snow off the leaf, the photo eyes and the solar panel.
3. **For the plow.** Use the hold-open so the gate is fully open and stays there.
4. **In a thaw.** Watch for water pooling at the gate. That is tomorrow's ice.

## When it is frozen

- **Do not keep pressing the remote.** The operator will try, stall and try again. That is how gearboxes and arms get damaged.
- **Use the manual release.** Disconnect the operator and work the gate by hand.
- **Find what is holding it.** Ice at the bottom, a frozen latch, packed snow in the arc.
- **Clear it gently.** Shovel and brush. Avoid prying on the gate or pouring hot water that will refreeze.
- **Re-engage and test.** Check that the safety devices are clear and working.

If the gate binds with no snow or ice in the way, something has moved. That is a repair, not a weather problem. See [repairs and upgrades](/gates/gate-repairs-and-upgrades/).
