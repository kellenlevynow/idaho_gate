## The four materials

### Steel

Mild steel is the workhorse of gate fabrication. It is strong and stiff, which keeps long leaves flat. It welds easily, forms well and costs less than the alternatives. Tube, bar, plate and sheet are all readily available, so almost any design can be built in it.

Its one weakness is rust. Bare steel outdoors begins to corrode at once. Every steel gate depends on its finish.

**Best for:** most driveway and estate gates, anything heavy-duty, ornamental work, structural posts.

### Aluminum

Aluminum weighs roughly a third as much as steel. It does not rust: it forms a thin, stable oxide skin instead. That makes it a natural fit near water and anywhere weight matters.

It is also less stiff. In the same shape, an aluminum member deflects about three times as much as steel under the same load. So aluminum gates use deeper sections, thicker walls or more bracing. Done properly, the result is a light leaf that is easy on hinges, posts and operators.

**Best for:** wide leaves, solar-powered gates, waterfront properties, railings over stone or decking.

### Weathering steel

Often known by the trade name Corten. It is a steel alloy designed to form a tight, stable rust layer that protects the metal beneath, so it needs no paint or powder. The color deepens from orange to a dark brown over a few seasons.

Two cautions. Runoff during the first years will stain concrete, pavers and light stone below. And it needs to dry out between wettings, so details must drain and avoid standing water.

**Best for:** panels, slats and feature elements on modern and rural properties where runoff is planned for.

### Timber in a steel frame

Wood brings warmth that metal cannot. On its own, a wide wooden gate sags and twists. Inside a welded steel frame, the wood becomes infill and the steel does the structural work.

Wood moves with moisture and needs refinishing. Fixings should let boards expand and shrink, and the bottom edge should be kept clear of splash and snow.

**Best for:** privacy gates that need to match timber on a house.

## Finishes for steel

### Powder coat

Dry powder is applied electrostatically and baked into a hard, even film. It is tougher than brushed paint and comes in a wide range of colors and textures. The standard we recommend is abrasive blasting, a zinc-rich primer, then the color coat.

### Hot-dip galvanizing

The finished steel is dipped in molten zinc, which bonds to the surface inside and out, including inside tube. Zinc protects steel even at a scratch, because it corrodes in place of the steel. Galvanized steel can be left as is, with a gray metallic look, or powder coated over.

### Galvanizing plus powder coat

Called a duplex system. The zinc protects the steel. The powder protects the zinc and gives color. Each makes the other last longer. For gates that will see de-icing salt, sprinklers or hard use, it is the best finish available.

### Liquid paint

Good industrial paint systems work, and they are easy to touch up in the field. They generally need more maintenance than powder or galvanizing. We use paint mainly for repairs and field welds.

## Choosing for North Idaho

- **Freeze and thaw.** Water that gets into a seam or an open tube will freeze and open it further. Seal-weld joints or provide drainage.
- **Snow contact.** The bottom of a gate sits in snow for months. That is where finishes fail first.
- **De-icing chemicals.** Splash from treated roads is corrosive.
- **Sun.** High-altitude summer sun fades some colors. Dark, UV-stable powders hold up well.
