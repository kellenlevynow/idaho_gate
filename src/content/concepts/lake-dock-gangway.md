## The brief we set ourselves

A walkway from a fixed shore abutment to a floating dock. The water level changes seasonally, so the angle of the gangway changes with it. It must be light enough to handle, stiff enough not to bounce, and need no painting.

## The design response

**Aluminum.** It does not rust, it is a third the weight of steel, and bare aluminum has no coating to fail in a wet environment.

**The handrail is the structure.** The side rails are trusses. Top chord, bottom chord and diagonals form a deep beam on each side, so the gangway spans without heavy stringers. The part you hold is the part that holds you.

**Hinged at shore, rolling on the float.** One end pivots. The other rides on rollers so it can travel as the dock rises, falls and shifts.

## Details that matter

- **Deck surface** with real grip when wet.
- **Toe rails** so nothing rolls off the edge.
- **A transition plate** at the float end so there is no gap or lip.
- **Isolation** between aluminum and any steel hardware to prevent corrosion where the two meet.
- **Welds placed and sized** with aluminum's behavior near a weld in mind.

## Sizing

Span follows the distance to the float at the lowest water level, and the steepest acceptable slope at that level. Width follows use: a private dock and a public marina are different. Loads and any accessibility requirements for public facilities come from the code and the design professional on the project.

## Approvals

Docks and shoreline structures are commonly regulated. Before fabrication, confirm what the state, county or lake authority allows at your site.

## What would change on a real site

Span, width, the shore connection and the float it lands on. Everything else follows from those.
