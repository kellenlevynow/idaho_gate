## Specified for duty, not looks alone

Three things separate a commercial gate from a residential one.

- **Cycles.** A storage facility or a fleet yard can cycle a gate hundreds of times a day. Operators are rated by duty cycle. A residential operator on a commercial entrance fails early.
- **Width and weight.** Truck access means 20 feet and up. That pushes most sites to cantilever slide gates with heavy bottom rails and properly braced tails.
- **Liability.** Public and employee traffic near a moving gate raises the bar on safety devices, guarding and signage.

We start a commercial gate by asking who uses it, how often, in what vehicles, and who needs to be kept out.

## What a complete entrance includes

1. **The gate.** Frame, infill, rollers or hinges, receiver, finish.
2. **Foundations.** Post footings and an operator pad, designed for the gate loads and the soil.
3. **The operator.** Sized for weight, width and duty cycle, with battery backup where the site needs it.
4. **Entrapment protection.** Monitored photo eyes and edges in both directions of travel.
5. **Vehicle detection.** Loops or sensors for free exit, safety and shadow.
6. **Access control.** Credentials for staff, a call path for visitors, a log if you need one.
7. **Emergency access.** Whatever the fire code official requires.
8. **Protection.** Bollards for the operator and pedestals.

Miss one and the gate becomes the weak point of the site. The plan drawing on this page shows the layout for a concept slide gate.

## Security without a fortress

Security comes from layers: a gate that is hard to climb and hard to force, an operator that locks when closed, lighting, and controlled credentials. It does not have to look hostile. Close picket spacing in a clean frame reads as architecture. Where a higher rating is needed, we discuss it plainly and bring in the right specialists.
