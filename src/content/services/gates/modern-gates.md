## Clean lines are a tolerance problem

On a traditional picket gate, the eye forgives a sixteenth of an inch. On a modern gate with twelve horizontal slats and a reveal you can count, it does not. A slat that wanders shows from the street. A frame that pulled out of square during welding shows every time the gate closes against its post.

We build modern gates on fixtures. Slats are spaced with gauge blocks, not by eye. Welds are sequenced to manage heat so the frame stays flat. Where the design calls for it, slats attach from behind so the face stays clean.

## Choosing the infill

- **Open slats** keep air and light moving and take less wind.
- **Tight slats** give near-full privacy and still drain and breathe.
- **Flush sheet** is fully private, heaviest in wind, and shows off a finish.
- **Timber in steel** brings warmth. Wood moves with the seasons, so we detail it to move without loosening.

## Why modern gates so often slide

A long, low, horizontal gate suits a cantilever slide. The whole panel moves along the fence line, so there is no swing arc to keep clear of snow or parked cars, and wind load goes into two posts instead of one hinge. If you have the run-back room, it is usually the cleanest answer. Compare the two in [swing vs sliding gates](/resources/swing-vs-sliding-gates/).

## Finish is half the design

Matte black powder coat is the default for a reason. Graphite metallic reads as machined steel. Dark bronze sits well with cedar. Weathering steel is its own statement. You can compare all of them on the same panel in the [gate studio](/studio/) and read the trade-offs in [materials and finishes](/company/materials-finishes/).

## Details we sweat

- Hinges and operator arms set behind the plane of the gate where possible.
- Drain holes and open ends so tube does not hold water and freeze.
- Posts that match the gate's proportions, not a stock size.
- A latch side that closes against a clean reveal, not a bolted-on stop.
